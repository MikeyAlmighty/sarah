#+title: Logical Equivalence of Sentences in FOL

* Logical Equivalence of Sentences in FOL

** Logical equivalence

Let $L$ be a first-order language and $X, Y$ sentences in $L$.

Then $X$ is *logically equivalent* to $Y$, written $X \equiv Y$, if for every interpretation $I$ of $L$ in a structure $\mathbf{D}$:
1. if $I(X)$ is *true* then $I(Y)$ is *true*
2. if $I(X)$ is *false* then $I(Y)$ is *false*

Having defined *logical equivalence* for FOL, we can now state *De Morgan's laws* for FOL.

** De Morgan's laws for Quantifiers

*De Morgan's* laws are an important and useful example of a [[id:24cbbc6b-858b-4e55-963e-66fedd470bca][Logical Equivalence]] in PL.

In PL, De Morgan *negates* conjunctions/disjunctions.

In FOL, it tells us how to *negate* $(\forall x)$ and $(\exists x)$.

*** Theorem 6.1 (De Morgan for quantifiers)

Assume $\neg(\forall x)X$ and $\neg(\exists x)X$ are sentences.
1. $\neg(\forall x)X \equiv (\exists x)\neg X$
2. $\neg(\exists x)X \equiv (\forall x)\neg X$

** Example: "all cows like grass"

- Domain $D$ = the set of all animals.
  - We want to say "all cows like grass".
- $C(x)$: 1-place *predicate* symbol, interpreted as the *unary* relation "$x$ is a cow"
- $G(x)$: 1-place *predicate* symbol, interpreted as the *unary* relation "$x$ likes grass"

Formalisation: $(\forall x)(C(x) \to G(x))$

Reads as "for every animal $x$, if $x$ is a cow then $x$ likes grass".

In other words, there is no animal $x$ that is a cow but does not like grass.

*** Negation:

\begin{align*}
\neg(\forall x)(C(x) \to G(x)) &\equiv (\exists x)\neg(C(x) \to G(x)) && \text{De Morgan for Quantifiers}\\
&\equiv (\exists x)\neg(\neg C(x) \lor G(x)) && x \to y \equiv \neg x \lor y\\
&\equiv (\exists x)(C(x) \land \neg G(x)) && \text{double negation + De Morgan}
\end{align*}

Reading: "there is a cow which does not like grass", the *negation* of "all cows like grass".
