#+title: Introduction to Recurrence Relations

* Introduction to Recurrence Relations

** Definition

A *recurrence relation* is an equation in which the $n$ -th term of a *sequence* depends on a /combination/ of /previous terms/ of the *sequence*.

** Basic example: $a_n = 2a_{n-1}$, $n \geq 2$

- Shorthand for *infinitely* many equations:

\begin{equation*}
\begin{cases}
a_2 = 2a_1, \\
a_3 = 2a_2, \\
a_4 = 2a_3, \\
\qquad \vdots
\end{cases}
\end{equation*}

- A /solution/ is a *sequence* $a = (a_1, a_2, a_3, \dots)$ that makes each of the above equations *true*.
  - In other words, each term of the *sequence* is /twice/ as big as the previous one.
  - $n \geq 2$ means the first element of the sequence is indexed by $1$, so $a_1$ is the first element.
- There are /many/ such *sequences*, each called a *solution* of the recurrence relation. For example:
  \[ a = (1, 2, 4, 8, 16, \dots) \quad \text{and} \quad b = (3, 6, 12, 24, 48, \dots) \]
  - Indeed, for $b$, say, we have
    \[ b_1 = 3, \quad b_2 = 6 = 2 \times 3 = 2b_1, \quad b_3 = 12 = 2 \times 6 = 2b_2, \quad \text{etc.} \]
- As soon as we choose the first element ($a_1$ or $b_1$), the whole sequence is /fixed/: there is a *unique* solution.
  - We call $a_1$ (or $b_1$) an *initial condition* of the recurrence relation.

** A more complicated example: $a_n = a_{n-1} + a_{n-2}$, $n \geq 3$

- The $n$ -th term depends on a combination of the previous terms $a_{n-1}$ and $a_{n-2}$.
- The sequence starts at $a_1$, since $n \geq 3$ and the term with the lowest index is $a_{n-2}$.
- Plugging in values of $n$:
  - $n = 3$: $a_3 = a_2 + a_1$
  - $n = 4$: $a_4 = a_3 + a_2$
  - $n = 5$: $a_5 = a_4 + a_3$
- $a_3$ depends on $a_1$ and $a_2$, which are not specified, so there are /many/ solutions, one for each pair $(a_1, a_2)$:
  - $a_1 = 1$, $a_2 = 2$ gives $a_3 = 3$, $a_4 = 5$, ...
  - $a_1 = a_2 = 0$ gives the zero sequence $(0, 0, 0, \dots)$.

** Initial conditions

- To get a *unique* solution for $a_n = a_{n-1} + a_{n-2}$, $n \geq 3$, we must specify $a_1$ and $a_2$.
  - These numbers are called *initial conditions*.
  - Only once they are specified can we compute $a_3$, then $a_4$, etc.
- A *solution of a recurrence relation* consists of /infinitely/ many numbers.
  - Compare equations like $3x + 4 = 2$ or $x^2 + 2x - 1 = 0$, which have only /finitely/ many solutions.
- We can't write down /infinitely/ many numbers, so a solution is a *formula* of the form
  \[ a_n = f(n), \]
  where $f$ is some function depending on $n$.
  - This completely captures the *solution sequence*: for any $n$ we can compute the $n$ -th term $a_n$ directly.
  - $a_n$ is /no longer/ expressed in terms of its predecessors, as it is in
    \[ a_n = a_{n-1} + a_{n-2}, \quad n \geq 3. \]

*** General solution

- The *general solution* is a solution that does /not/ use the initial conditions.
- For $a_n = 2a_{n-1}$, $n \geq 2$:
  \[ a_n = a_1 \cdot 2^{n-1}, \quad n \geq 1. \]
  - For each choice of the initial condition $a_1$, we get a *unique* sequence.
  - Derived in the next lesson, on *linear homogeneous recurrence relations*.
- If *initial conditions* are given, substitute them into the *general solution* to get the *unique solution*.

*** How many initial conditions?

- $a_n = 2a_{n-1} + 1$, $n \geq 2$: needs *one initial value* to find $a_n$ for all $n \geq 2$.
  - With $a_1 = 1$: $a_2 = 3$, $a_3 = 7$.
- $a_n = 5a_{n-1} - 6a_{n-2}$, $n \geq 3$: needs *two initial values* to find $a_n$ for all $n \geq 3$.
  - With $a_1 = 1$ and $a_2 = 5$: $a_3 = 19$, $a_4 = 65$.

** Example: verifying a solution

Verify that the solution of $a_n = 2a_{n-1} + 1$, $n \geq 2$, with $a_1 = 1$ is $a_n = 2^n - 1$.

*Solution.* We have to do two things:
- (Task 1): Check that the given formula gives the correct *initial value*.
- (Task 2): Check that the given formula /solves/ the *equation* (*Recurrence Relation*).

*** *Task 1.*

Putting $n = 1$ in $a_n = 2^n - 1$ gives $a_1 = 2 - 1 = 1$, so the first task is completed.

*** *Task 2.*

Evaluate - the *recurrence relation* - $2a_{n-1} + 1$ using the given formula and show that it is equal to $a_n$.
- Now, $a_{n-1} = 2^{n-1} - 1$, so
  \[ 2a_{n-1} + 1 = 2(2^{n-1} - 1) + 1 = 2^n - 1, \]
- and hence $a_n = 2a_{n-1} + 1$.
