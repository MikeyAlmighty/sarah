Mathematical Contradiction

  • Proof by Contradiction

Idea

To prove a statement \(S\) is true, assume \(S\) is false and show this leads to something impossible (a contradiction). Since the assumption breaks logic, it must be wrong — so \(S\) must be true.

The pattern

  1. Assume \(\neg S\) (the negation of what you want to prove).
  2. Reason validly from that assumption.
  3. Derive a contradiction — two statements that can't both be true (e.g. \(p_1 \mid P\) and \(p_1 \nmid P\), or \(1 = 0\), or an object that's both smaller and bigger than itself).
  4. Conclude \(\neg S\) is false, so \(S\) is true.

Why it works

Built on the law of excluded middle: a statement is either true or false, nothing in between. If assuming false breaks logic, true is the only option left.

Common shapes it takes

  • Direct contradiction: assume \(\neg S\), derive \(A\) and \(\neg A\) for some statement \(A\).
  • Minimal counterexample / infinite descent: assume a counterexample exists, take the smallest one, show a smaller one must also exist — contradiction. See Minimal Counterexample / Infinite Descent.

Contradiction vs. contrapositive

Easy to confuse:

  • Contrapositive: to prove "if \(A\) then \(B\)," prove "if not \(B\) then not \(A\)" instead. Still a direct, constructive argument — no contradiction involved.
  • Contradiction: assume \(A\) and not \(B\) together, derive nonsense.

Contradiction is the more general tool; contrapositive is really a special case of it.
