Rational Numbers \(\mathbb{Q}\)

A rational number is a number that can be written as a fraction:

\[ \frac{p}{q} \]

where:

  • \(p \in \mathbb{Z}\) (the numerator)
  • \(q \in \mathbb{Z}\) (the denominator)
  • \(q \neq 0\)

The set of rational numbers is denoted by \(\mathbb{Q}\) (Quotient)

Examples of rational numbers:

  • \(\frac{1}{2}\)
  • \(-\frac{3}{4}\)
  • \(5 = \frac{5}{1}\)
  • \(0 = \frac{0}{1}\)

Non-Uniqueness of Representation

A rational number can often be represented in many different ways.

For example:

\[ \frac{1}{2} = \frac{2}{4} = \frac{3}{6} \]

These fractions all represent the same rational number.

To obtain a unique representation, we require:

  • \(q > 0\)
  • the fraction is in lowest terms

A fraction is in lowest terms if:

  • the numerator and denominator share no common factors

For example:

  • \(\frac{2}{4}\) is not in lowest terms
  • \(\frac{1}{2}\) is in lowest terms

Why We Introduce Rational Numbers

The integers \( \mathbb{Z} \) are not closed under division.

For example:

\[ 1 \div 2 = \frac{1}{2} \]

but:

\[ \frac{1}{2} \notin \mathbb{Z} \]

By extending \( \mathbb{Z} \) to \( \mathbb{Q} \), division becomes possible whenever the divisor is non-zero.

Thus, in \( \mathbb{Q} \), we can:

  • add
  • subtract
  • multiply
  • divide (except by zero)

Field Structure

The rational numbers form a field.

A field is a number system where:

  • addition
  • subtraction
  • multiplication
  • division (by non-zero elements)

are all well-defined and closed.

The rational numbers are the standard example of a field in algebra.

Ordering of Rational Numbers

The rational numbers admit an ordering \(<\).

Given:

\[ \frac{p_1}{q_1}, \frac{p_2}{q_2} \]

with:

\[ q_1, q_2 > 0 \]

we define:

\[ \frac{p_1}{q_1} < \frac{p_2}{q_2} \]

if:

\[ p_1 q_2 < p_2 q_1 \]

This allows rational numbers to be compared without converting them to decimals.

Example:

\[ \frac{1}{2} < \frac{3}{4} \]

because:

\[ 1 \times 4 < 3 \times 2 \]

that is:

\[ 4 < 6 \]

Density of the Rational Numbers

Unlike \( \mathbb{Z} \), the rational numbers do not have a unique next element.

Between any two rational numbers, there is always another rational number.

We say that the ordering of \( \mathbb{Q} \) is dense.

For example, between:

\[ \frac{1}{2} \quad \text{and} \quad \frac{3}{4} \]

we can find:

\[ \frac{5}{8} \]

More generally, if:

\[ a_1 < a_2 \]

then the average:

\[ \frac{a_1 + a_2}{2} \]

lies strictly between them.

Since rational numbers are closed under addition and division by non-zero integers:

  • this average is also rational

Thus:

  • there is always another rational number between two rational numbers

Loss of Successor Structure

In \( \mathbb{N} \):

  • every number has a unique successor

In \( \mathbb{Q} \):

  • no rational number has an immediate next rational number

This means:

  • the discrete ordering of \( \mathbb{N} \) and \( \mathbb{Z} \) is lost

The rational numbers are instead densely ordered.

Why Division by Zero is Undefined

Division is defined as the inverse of multiplication.

So:

\[ z = \frac{x}{y} \]

means:

\[ z \times y = x \]

Suppose we attempted to divide by zero:

\[ z = \frac{x}{0} \]

Then we would require:

\[ z \times 0 = x \]

But for every number \(z\):

\[ z \times 0 = 0 \]

So this equation is only possible if:

\[ x = 0 \]

Even then, we encounter a problem.

For:

\[ \frac{0}{0} \]

we would need a number \(z\) satisfying:

\[ z \times 0 = 0 \]

But every number satisfies this condition.

Thus:

  • there is no unique answer
  • division by zero is undefined

Therefore:

  • division by zero is considered meaningless in standard arithmetic.
