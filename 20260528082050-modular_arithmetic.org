#+title: Modular Arithmetic

* Modular Arithmetic

** Modular Notation

To put the focus on the *remainder* \(r\) rather than the *quotient* \(q\), we write:

\[ b \mod a = r \]

We read this as "\(r\) equals \(b\) modulo \(a\)". It tells us there exists
some integer \(q\) such that \(b = a \cdot q + r\). This is especially
important in *cryptology* and *computer science*, where the *remainder* is
often /more useful/ than the *quotient*.

** Examples

\[ 11 = 3 \times 3 + 2 \implies 11 \mod 3 = 2 \]

| Dividend | Divisor | Quotient | Remainder |
|----------+---------+----------+-----------|
| 11       | 3       | 3        | 2         |

\[ 17 = 3 \times 5 + 2 \implies 17 \mod 3 = 2 \]

| Dividend | Divisor | Quotient | Remainder |
|----------+---------+----------+-----------|
| 17       | 3       | 5        | 2         |

Since \(11\) and \(17\) have the same remainder when divided by \(3\):

\[ 11 \mod 3 = 17 \mod 3 \]

We can also note that \(2 \mod 3 = 2\) since \(2 = 3 \times 0 + 2\), giving us:

\[ 11 \mod 3 = 17 \mod 3 = 2 \mod 3 \]

** Key Property

If \(m\) divides the difference \(a - b\), then:

\[ a \mod m = b \mod m \]

This lets us check equality of remainders *without* computing them directly.
For example, \(17 - 11 = 6\) is divisible by \(3\), so we immediately know
\(17 \mod 3 = 11 \mod 3\) without doing the division.

** Notational Warning

The notation \(11 = 2 \mod 3\) does *not* mean "the remainder when dividing
\(2\) by \(3\) is \(11\)". It is shorthand for:

\[ 11 \mod 3 = 2 \mod 3 \]

i.e. that \(11\) and \(2\) have the same remainder when divided by \(3\).
