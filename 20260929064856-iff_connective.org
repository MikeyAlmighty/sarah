#+title: IFF Connective

* IFF Connective

- aka *Biconditional*

** Definition
The connective *iff* (written $p \leftrightarrow q$ or $p \iff q$) is a *binary connective*.

It reads "$p$ if and only if $q$" or "$p$ is equivalent to $q$".

The compound statement is ~true~ when $p$ and $q$ have the /same/ truth value (both ~true~ or both ~false~).

** Truth table

| $p$ | $q$ | $p \leftrightarrow q$ |
|-----+-----+-----------------------|
| T   | T   | T                     |
| T   | F   | F                     |
| F   | T   | F                     |
| F   | F   | T                     |

** Example: not(p iff q)

Add a column for the negation of the whole statement (the brackets matter):

| $p$ | $q$ | $p \leftrightarrow q$ | $\lnot(p \leftrightarrow q)$ |
|-----+-----+-----------------------+------------------------------|
| T   | T   | T                     | F                            |
| T   | F   | F                     | T                            |
| F   | T   | F                     | T                            |
| F   | F   | T                     | F                            |

Dropping the intermediate column gives the table for $\lnot(p \leftrightarrow q)$, which is *exactly the same* as the table for [[id:791af3eb-ea5c-4458-85c8-9e7c93edf32f][XOR]].

- Hence $\lnot(p \leftrightarrow q) \equiv p \oplus q$.
- $\lnot p \leftrightarrow q$ (no brackets) means something different: "not $p$ is equivalent to $q$".

** Terminology

- *Logically equivalent*: two compound statements that give the /same/ truth values for the same truth values of $p$ and $q$.

In PL they "mean the same thing".

** Notes

- Commutative: $p \leftrightarrow q \equiv q \leftrightarrow p$.
- =iff= is the opposite of =xor=: ~true~ when the inputs *agree*, whereas =xor= is ~true~ when they *differ*.
