Verifying a Solution to a Recurrence Relation

The method

Given a recurrence with initial condition(s) and a candidate closed form \(a_n = f(n)\), check two things:

  1. Initial values: \(f\) gives the correct value for each initial condition.
  2. Recurrence: substituting \(f\) into the right-hand side of the recurrence gives \(f(n)\).

Why Both Checks are Enough

  • The recurrence and its initial conditions determine exactly one sequence:
    • The initial values are fixed, and every later term is computed from earlier ones.
  • If \(f\) matches the initial values and satisfies the recurrence, it produces that same sequence, term by term.
    • This is an induction argument: the initial values are the base case and the recurrence check is the inductive step.
  • Skipping check 1 only shows that \(f\) is some solution, not the one you want.
    • For example, \(a_n = 2^n\) satisfies \(a_n = 2a_{n-1}\) but has \(a_1 = 2\), not \(a_1 = 1\).

Example 1: one initial condition

Claim: \(a_n = 2^n - 1\) solves \(a_n = 2a_{n-1} + 1\), \(n \geq 2\), with \(a_1 = 1\).

  1. Initial value: \(a_1 = 2^1 - 1 = 1\). ✓
  2. Recurrence: \(a_{n-1} = 2^{n-1} - 1\), so \[ 2a_{n-1} + 1 = 2(2^{n-1} - 1) + 1 = 2^n - 2 + 1 = 2^n - 1 = a_n. \] ✓

Example 2: the general solution

Claim: \(a_n = a_1 \cdot 2^{n-1}\) solves \(a_n = 2a_{n-1}\), \(n \geq 2\), for any choice of \(a_1\).

  1. Initial value: \(a_1 \cdot 2^{1-1} = a_1 \cdot 1 = a_1\). ✓
  2. Recurrence: \(2a_{n-1} = 2 \cdot a_1 \cdot 2^{n-2} = a_1 \cdot 2^{n-1} = a_n\). ✓

Example 3: two initial conditions

Claim: \(a_n = 3^n - 2^n\) solves \(a_n = 5a_{n-1} - 6a_{n-2}\), \(n \geq 3\), with \(a_1 = 1\), \(a_2 = 5\).

  1. Initial values: \(a_1 = 3 - 2 = 1\) ✓ and \(a_2 = 9 - 4 = 5\) ✓. Both must be checked.

  2. Recurrence:

    \begin{align*} 5a_{n-1} - 6a_{n-2} &= 5(3^{n-1} - 2^{n-1}) - 6(3^{n-2} - 2^{n-2}) \\ &= 15 \cdot 3^{n-2} - 10 \cdot 2^{n-2} - 6 \cdot 3^{n-2} + 6 \cdot 2^{n-2} \\ &= 9 \cdot 3^{n-2} - 4 \cdot 2^{n-2} \\ &= 3^n - 2^n = a_n. \end{align*}

    ✓

Sanity check against the computed terms: \(a_3 = 27 - 8 = 19\) and \(a_4 = 81 - 16 = 65\).

Tip: write every power in terms of the lowest one (\(3^{n-2}\), \(2^{n-2}\)) so the terms can be collected.

Example 4: A Candidate That Fails

Claim: \(a_n = 2n - 1\) solves \(a_n = 2a_{n-1} + 1\) with \(a_1 = 1\).

  • First terms: \(f(1) = 1\) ✓, \(f(2) = 3\) ✓, \(f(3) = 5\), but the recurrence gives \(a_3 = 7\). ✗
  • Algebraically: \(2a_{n-1} + 1 = 2(2(n-1) - 1) + 1 = 4n - 5 \neq 2n - 1\) in general.
  • Lesson: matching the first few terms proves nothing.
    • The recurrence check has to be done for general \(n\).
