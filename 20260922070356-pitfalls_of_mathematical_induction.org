#+title: Pitfalls of Mathematical Induction

* Pitfalls of Mathematical Induction

** Overview

To prove a statement by induction you must carry out *all* of the following steps:

- State $P(n)$.
- Show that $P(1)$ is ~true~ (base case).
- Do the inductive step: show that if $P(k)$ is ~true~ then $P(k+1)$
  is ~true~.

Skipping any one of these can produce a ~false~ "proof."
The three examples below each omit exactly one step.

** Pitfall 1: only proving the base case

It does not suffice to prove only that $P(1)$ is true.

- Let $P(n)$ be the statement $n^2 = n$.
- $P(1)$ is true.
- But $P(n)$ only holds for $n = 0$ or $n = 1$, and is false for
  $n \geq 2$.

/Missing step: the inductive step./

** Pitfall 2: only proving the inductive step

It does not suffice to prove only that $P(k) \implies P(k+1)$.

- Let $P(n)$ be the statement $n + 1 = n$.
- Assume $P(k)$ holds, i.e. $k + 1 = k$. Adding $1$ to both sides
  gives $k + 2 = k + 1$, i.e. $P(k+1)$.
- So the inductive step goes through vacuously, but $P(1)$ is
  false, so induction never gets started.

/Missing step: the base case./ (Here it's vacuous since no $P(k)$
is ever actually true.)

** Pitfall 3: inductive step only valid for some $k$

A more subtle error: proving $P(k) \implies P(k+1)$ but only for
some values of $k$ (e.g. $k \geq 2$), while implicitly claiming it
for all $k$.

- Let $P(n)$ be: any $n$ students taking the same exam score the
  same mark.
- $P(1)$ is clearly true.
- *"Proof" of inductive step:* Assume $P(k)$. Take a group of
  $k+1$ candidates. Let $A, B$ be two particular candidates and
  $C$ the remaining $k - 1$ students.
  - $A \cup C$ is a group of $k$ students, so by $P(k)$ they all
    share a mark.
  - $B \cup C$ is likewise a group of $k$ students sharing a mark.
  - Since $C$ is common to both, $A$, $B$, and $C$ all share the
    same mark, so $P(k+1)$ holds.
- *The flaw:* this argument requires $C$ to be non-empty so that
  the two groups' marks are forced to agree. $C$ has $k - 1$
  students, so when $k = 1$, $C$ is empty and the argument breaks
  down. We have really only shown $P(k) \implies P(k+1)$ for
  $k \geq 2$ — there is no valid link from $P(1)$ to $P(2)$, and
  $P(2)$ is false.

** Takeaway

When writing an [[id:8b1e8e5b-d981-41e0-93a7-eded048cd150][Induction Proof]], explicitly check:

1. Does the *base case* really hold?
2. Does the *inductive step* hold for *every* relevant $k$ (not just large $k$), and does it actually use $P(k)$ correctly?
3. Does the chain from the base case actually connect to the *inductive step* (no implicit non-emptiness / edge-case assumptions)?
