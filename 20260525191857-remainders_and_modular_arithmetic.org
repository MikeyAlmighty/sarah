#+title: Remainders and Modular Arithmetic

* Remainders and Modular Arithmetic

** Division with Remainders

For any positive integers \(a\) and \(b\), we can always write:

\[ b = a \cdot q + r \]

where \(0 \leq r < a\) is the *remainder*.

If \(r = 0\) then \(a \mid b\).

For example, with \(b = 14\) and \(a = 3\):

\[ 14 = 3 \cdot 4 + 2 \]

where \(4\) is the *quotient* and \(2\) is the *remainder*.

** Modular Notation

To put the focus on the *remainder* \(r\) rather than the *quotient* \(q\), we write:

\[ b \mod a = r \]

We read this as "\(r\) equals \(b\) modulo \(a\)".

It tells us there exists some integer \(q\) such that \(b = a \cdot q + r\).

This is especially important in *cryptology* and *computer science*, where the *remainder* is often /more useful/ than the *quotient*.

** Examples

\[ 11 \mod 3 = 2 \quad \text{since} \quad 11 = 3 \times 3 + 2 \]

\[ 17 \mod 3 = 2 \quad \text{since} \quad 17 = 3 \times 5 + 2 \]

Since \(11\) and \(17\) have the same remainder when divided by \(3\), we can write:

\[ 11 \mod 3 = 17 \mod 3 \].

We can also note that \(2 \mod 3 = 2\) since \(2 = 3 \times 0 + 2\), giving us:

\[ 11 \mod 3 = 17 \mod 3 = 2 \mod 3 \].

In general, if \(m\) divides the difference \(a - b\), then:

\[ a \mod m = b \mod m \]

This lets us verify \(11 \mod 3 = 17 \mod 3\) without computing remainders
at all — since \(17 - 11 = 6\) is divisible by \(3\).

** Key Property

If \(m\) divides the difference \(a - b\), then:

\[ a \mod m = b \mod m \]

This lets us check equality of remainders *without* computing them directly.
For example, \(17 - 11 = 6\) is divisible by \(3\), so we immediately know
\(17 \mod 3 = 11 \mod 3\) without doing the division.

** Notational Warning

The notation \(11 = 2 \mod 3\) does *not* mean "the remainder when dividing
\(2\) by \(3\) is \(11\)". It is shorthand for:

\[ 11 \mod 3 = 2 \mod 3 \]

i.e. that \(11\) and \(2\) have the same remainder when divided by \(3\).
