#+title: Fundamental Theorem of Algebra

* Fundamental Theorem of Algebra

** Statement

Every non-constant polynomial with coefficients in ℂ has at least one root
in ℂ. Equivalently, every polynomial of degree n has *exactly n roots* in ℂ,
counted with multiplicity.

** Consequence: ℂ is Algebraically Closed

This is precisely what it means for ℂ to be *algebraically closed* — you
never need to extend the number system further to find a root. No such
guarantee exists for ℕ, ℤ, ℚ, or ℝ:

| System | x² + 1 = 0 solvable? |
|--------+----------------------|
| ℕ      | ✗                    |
| ℤ      | ✗                    |
| ℚ      | ✗                    |
| ℝ      | ✗                    |
| ℂ      | ✓  (roots: ±i)       |

** Multiplicity

A root r has multiplicity m if (z - r)^m divides the polynomial but
(z - r)^(m+1) does not. For example:

#+begin_example
z² - 2z + 1 = (z - 1)²
#+end_example

has one root, z = 1, with multiplicity 2. The theorem still counts this as
two roots.

** Connection to Complex Conjugates

For polynomials with *real* coefficients, complex roots always appear as [[id:a72e23a6-06f2-4e4d-b163-6571c9abb09d][conjugate pairs]] - if a + bi is a root, so is a - bi. This is why quadratics
with real coefficients either have two real roots or two complex roots; never
just one complex root.

** Historical Note

The theorem was first rigorously proved by *Gauss* in his 1799 doctoral
dissertation, though earlier incomplete proofs had been attempted. Gauss
went on to give three further proofs during his lifetime.
