#+title: Mathematical Induction

* Examples

** Mathematical Induction — the three steps

1. *Base case.* Show $P(1)$ is true.
2. *Inductive step.* Assume $P(k)$ is true (for arbitrary positive
   integer $k$), and show $P(k+1)$ follows from it.
3. *Conclusion.* By induction, $P(n)$ is true for all positive
   integers $n$.

Key discipline: only manipulate the *LHS*.

RHS is the *target*, write it once (by substituting $n=k+1$ into the formula) and never
touch it again.

The proof is complete when algebra on the LHS lands exactly on that untouched RHS.

** Example: Sum Of Odd Numbers

Prove that for any positive integer $n$:
$$1 + 3 + 5 + 7 + \dots + (2n-1) = n^2$$

*** Statement $P(n)$

$$P(n): \quad 1+3+5+7+\dots+(2n-1) = n^2$$

*** Base case: $P(1)$

$$1 = 1^2$$

True (with $n=1$ the sum has only one term, the last term
$2(1)-1=1$). Hence $P(1)$ holds.

*** Inductive hypothesis: assume $P(k)$

$$1+3+5+7+\dots+(2k-1) = k^2$$

*** Inductive step: show $P(k+1)$ follows

Target ($P(k+1)$, obtained by substituting $n=k+1$):

$$1+3+5+7+\dots+(2k+1) = (k+1)^2$$

(the new last term is $2(k+1)-1 = 2k+1$ — the next odd number after
$2k-1$, since consecutive odd numbers differ by 2)

Work the LHS only:

$$1+3+5+\dots+(2k-1)+(2k+1) = \big[1+3+5+\dots+(2k-1)\big] + (2k+1)$$

(pure bracketing — no calculation yet)

Use the assumption $P(k)$ to replace the bracket with $k^2$:

$$= k^2 + 2k+1$$

Algebra (standard expansion, run in reverse):

$$k^2+2k+1 = (k+1)^2$$

This matches the target RHS exactly. $P(k+1)$ follows from $P(k)$.

*** Conclusion

By the principle of mathematical induction, $P(n)$ holds for every
positive integer $n$.

** Example: sum of first $n$ integers
Prove that for any positive integer $n$:
$$1+2+3+\dots+n = \frac{n(n+1)}{2}$$

*** Statement $P(n)$

$$P(n): \quad 1+2+3+\dots+n = \frac{n(n+1)}{2}$$

*** Base case: $P(1)$

$$1 = \frac{1(1+1)}{2} = \frac{2}{2} = 1$$

True. Hence $P(1)$ holds.

*** Inductive hypothesis: assume $P(k)$

$$1+2+3+\dots+k = \frac{k(k+1)}{2}$$

*** Inductive step: show $P(k+1)$ follows

Target ($P(k+1)$, substitute $n=k+1$ into both sides):

- LHS: $1+2+3+\dots+k+(k+1)$ — the sum extended by the next
  integer, $(k+1)$ (not "+1" tacked on — the whole quantity $(k+1)$
  is the new term)
- RHS: $\dfrac{(k+1)\big((k+1)+1\big)}{2} = \dfrac{(k+1)(k+2)}{2}$

So the target is:

$$1+2+3+\dots+k+(k+1) = \frac{(k+1)(k+2)}{2}$$

Work the LHS only:

$$1+2+3+\dots+k+(k+1) = \big[1+2+3+\dots+k\big] + (k+1)$$

(pure bracketing)

Use the assumption $P(k)$ to replace the bracket:

$$= \frac{k(k+1)}{2} + (k+1)$$

Common denominator:

$$= \frac{k(k+1)}{2} + \frac{2(k+1)}{2} = \frac{k(k+1) + 2(k+1)}{2}$$

Factor out $(k+1)$ from the numerator:

$$= \frac{(k+1)(k+2)}{2}$$

This matches the target RHS exactly. $P(k+1)$ follows from $P(k)$.

*** Conclusion

By the principle of mathematical induction, $P(n)$ holds for every positive integer $n$.

** Summary — the LHS/RHS discipline

| Step | What happens                                                               |
|------+----------------------------------------------------------------------------|
|    1 | Write $P(k+1)$'s LHS and RHS by substituting $n=k+1$                       |
|    2 | Set the RHS aside — it is the fixed target, never modified                 |
|    3 | Bracket the LHS to isolate the $P(k)$-shaped part                          |
|    4 | Substitute $k^2$ (or $\frac{k(k+1)}{2}$, etc.) using the assumption $P(k)$ |
|    5 | Do algebra on the LHS until it equals the RHS exactly                      |
