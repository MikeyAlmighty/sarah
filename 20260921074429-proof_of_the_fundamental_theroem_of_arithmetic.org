#+title: Proof of the Fundamental Theroem of Arithmetic

* Proof of the Fundamental Theroem of Arithmetic

** Statement

Every integer $n > 1$ has a unique factorisation

$$n = p_1^{e_1} p_2^{e_2} \cdots p_\ell^{e_\ell}$$

where $p_1, \dots, p_\ell$ are primes and $e_1, \dots, e_\ell$ are positive integers (their powers).

*** Example

$$132 = 2^2 \cdot 3 \cdot 11$$

** Proof

The theorem has two parts: *existence* of a factorisation, and *uniqueness* of it.
Existence uses induction; uniqueness uses proof by contradiction ([[id:24c9a751-0b87-47b4-b22a-d81844f8351a][Minimal Counterexample / Infinite Descent]]).

*** Existence

Every integer $n > 1$ is either prime, or can be written as a product of smaller factors.

By strong induction on $n$:
- If $n$ is prime, it is its own factorisation.
- If $n$ is composite, $n = ab$ with $1 < a, b < n$. By the induction hypothesis, $a$ and $b$
  each factor into primes, so $n$ does too.

*** Uniqueness

Assume by contradiction that there are two factorisations of some integer:

$$n = p_1^{e_1} \cdots p_\ell^{e_\ell} = q_1^{f_1} \cdots q_k^{f_k}$$

Take $n$ to be the *smallest* integer for which this happens.

**** Pull out one copy of each smallest prime

Write $n = p_1 \cdot P = q_1 \cdot Q$, where

$$P = p_1^{e_1 - 1} p_2^{e_2} \cdots p_\ell^{e_\ell}, \qquad Q = q_1^{f_1 - 1} q_2^{f_2} \cdots q_k^{f_k}$$

(pulling one $p_1$ out of the stack of $e_1$ copies leaves $e_1 - 1$ copies; same for $q_1$).

Take $p_1$ to be the overall smallest prime appearing in either factorisation. If $p_1 = q_1$,
cancel it from both sides to get $P = Q$ — a smaller integer with two factorisations,
contradicting minimality of $n$. So assume $p_1 \ne q_1$ (WLOG $p_1 < q_1$).

**** Construct a smaller number divisible by both $p_1$ and $q_1$

$$n - p_1 q_1 = p_1 P - p_1 q_1 = p_1(P - q_1)$$
$$n - p_1 q_1 = q_1 Q - p_1 q_1 = q_1(Q - p_1)$$

So $n - p_1 q_1 = p_1(P - q_1) = q_1(Q - p_1)$.

Since every prime factor of $Q$ is $\geq q_1 > p_1$, we get $Q - p_1 \geq 1$, hence

$$n > n - p_1 q_1 = q_1(Q - p_1) \geq q_1 > p_1$$

**** Derive the contradiction

$n - p_1 q_1$ is a positive integer smaller than $n$, so (by minimality of $n$) it has a
*unique* factorisation. Both $p_1$ and $q_1$ divide it (shown above), so $p_1 q_1$ divides it
cleanly:

$$n - p_1 q_1 = p_1 q_1 m \quad \text{for some integer } m$$

Substituting into $p_1(P - q_1) = p_1 q_1 m$:

$$P - q_1 = q_1 m \implies P = q_1(m+1)$$

So $q_1 \mid P$. But $P = p_1^{e_1-1} p_2^{e_2} \cdots p_\ell^{e_\ell}$, whose only possible
prime factors are $p_1, \dots, p_\ell$ — none of which equal $q_1$ (else it would've been
cancelled earlier). So $q_1$ *cannot* divide $P$.

Contradiction. $\blacksquare$

No integer has two distinct prime factorisations - uniqueness holds.

** Key lemma used

*Euclid's Lemma*: if a prime $p$ divides a product $ab$, then $p \mid a$ or $p \mid b$.
(Used implicitly in the divisibility argument above; worth a separate note if not yet covered.)

** Relevance to CS

Underpins RSA encryption (unique factorisation, hard to find but well-defined), hashing
schemes, and any algorithm reasoning about integers via their prime structure.
