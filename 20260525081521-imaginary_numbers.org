#+title: Imaginary Numbers

* Imaginary Numbers

** From Imaginary Numbers to Complex Numbers

*** Overview

Real numbers have a fundamental property: the square of any real number is
always non-negative, i.e. x² ≥ 0 for all x ∈ ℝ. This means equations like
x² = -1 have *no real solution*.

Rather than treating this as a dead end, 16th-century mathematicians began
treating √(-1) as a useful object in its own right — giving rise to imaginary numbers.

*** Definition

An *imaginary number* is any number of the form:

#+begin_example
bi,  where b ∈ ℝ  and  i = √(-1),  i.e. i² = -1
#+end_example

The constant ~i~ is the fundamental imaginary unit.

*** Key Property

Using standard algebraic rules:

#+begin_example
(bi)² = b²i² = b²(-1) = -b²
#+end_example

This means every real number — positive *or* negative — now has a square root:

| Expression  | Result  |
|-------------+---------|
| √(-9)       | 3i      |
| √(-100)     | 10i     |
| √(-2)       | √2 · i  |

*** Motivation

- The equation x² = -1 has no solution in ℝ, but introducing ~i~ resolves this:
  - More broadly, imaginary numbers allow square roots of all negative reals, paving the way for [[id:fe99a514-0c3d-48bf-836b-91622b4c1cae][complex numbers]] of the form a + bi.
