Natural Numbers (\(\mathbb{N}\))

Natural Numbers (\(\mathbb{N}\))

The set of natural numbers is denoted by: \[ \mathbb{N} = \{0,1,2,3,\dots\} \]

Used to:

  • Count items
  • Label elements in a set
  • Describe quantity

We consider operations performed on pairs of natural numbers and ask whether the result is still a natural number.

Operations on Natural Numbers

The natural numbers are closed under:

  • Addition (Adding two natural numbers always gives another natural number.)
  • Multiplication (Multiplying two natural numbers always gives another natural number.)

For example:

\[ 2 + 5 = 7 \]

and

\[ 3 \times 4 = 12 \]

both remain inside \( \mathbb{N} \).

Closure

A set is said to be closed under an operation if performing that operation on elements of the set always produces another element of the same set.

Thus:

  • \( \mathbb{N} \) is closed under addition.
  • \( \mathbb{N} \) is closed under multiplication.

Subtraction

The natural numbers are not closed under subtraction.

For example:

\[ 2 - 5 = -3 \]

But:

\[ -3 \notin \mathbb{N} \]

To allow subtraction in general, we extend the natural numbers to include negative numbers.

This larger set is called the integers:

\[ \mathbb{Z} = \{\dots,-3,-2,-1,0,1,2,3,\dots\} \]

The symbol \( \mathbb{Z} \) comes from the German word Zahlen, meaning "numbers".

Division

The natural numbers are also not closed under division.

For example:

\[ 1 \div 2 = \frac{1}{2} \]

But:

\[ \frac{1}{2} \notin \mathbb{N} \]

To include results like these, we must expand our number system further to the rational numbers \( \mathbb{Q} \).

Other Properties of \( \mathbb{N} \)

Aside from the algebraic properties discussed earlier, there are several other important properties of the natural numbers \( \mathbb{N} \).

These properties relate to:

  • Ordering
  • Counting
  • Successor relationships
  • Mathematical induction

The concept of ordering is fundamental in mathematics and computer science. It provides a formal way to describe statements such as:

  • "this is less than that"
  • "this precedes that"

The first ordering usually encountered is the standard order on the natural numbers.

Examples:

  • \(2 < 3\)
  • \(10 > 5\)
  • "Does Sally have fewer cookies than Tom?"

The idea of one number being greater than or less than another is one of the basic intuitions behind number systems.

Associated with counting is the idea of:

  • A first element (often \(0\) or \(1\))
  • A process for obtaining the next element

In practice, we may count:

  • in ones
  • in tens
  • in hundreds
  • or using another subset of \( \mathbb{N} \)

However, the underlying pattern remains the same.

Successor Function

To describe these non-algebraic properties formally, we use the notion of a successor.

For a natural number \(n\), the successor of \(n\) is:

\[ S(n) = n + 1 \]

Using the successor function, we can define ordering.

We say that:

\[ m \leq n \]

if \(n\) can be obtained from \(m\) by applying the successor function \(S\) finitely many times (including zero times).

For example:

  • \(3 \leq 5\) because: \[ 3 \to 4 \to 5 \]
  • \(4 \leq 4\) because no applications of \(S\) are needed.

Connection to Mathematical Induction

Underlying the principle of mathematical induction is the fact that every natural number can be obtained from \(0\) by repeatedly applying the successor function a finite number of times.

That is:

\[ 0,\ S(0),\ S(S(0)),\ S(S(S(0))),\ \dots \]

This idea is deeply connected to:

  • Iteration
  • Recursion
  • Loops in computer programs

The connection between induction and repeated computation is fundamental in discrete mathematics and computer science.
