Applications of Recurrence Relations

Why recurrences appear in applications

Many quantities grow or shrink one step at a time:

  • A savings balance after another year.
  • The number of moves needed after the problem has been reduced by one disc.
  • The number of ways to finish a staircase after the last move.

A recurrence records this "next term from earlier terms" structure directly, and the initial conditions say where the process starts.

Once a closed form is found, any term can be computed without working through all the earlier ones.

Example 1: Compound interest

Setup: £100 is deposited in an account paying 4% interest, compounded annually.

Let \(a_n\) be the balance after \(n\) years.

  • The balance after year \(n\) is the balance after year \(n-1\) plus 4% of that balance, so \(a_n = 1.04\, a_{n-1}\) for \(n \geq 1\).
  • Initial condition: \(a_0 = 100\).
    • Note that this sequence is indexed from \(n = 0\).
  • Check: \(a_1 = 1.04 \times 100 = 104\)
    • Which is the original £100 plus £4 of interest.
  • Unrolling the recurrence gives \(a_n = 100(1.04)^n\)
  • Verification with the method from Verifying a Solution to a Recurrence Relation:
    • Initial value (Task 1): \(100(1.04)^0 = 100\). ✓
    • Recurrence (Task 2): \(1.04 \cdot 100(1.04)^{n-1} = 100(1.04)^n = a_n\). ✓
Year \(n\) Start of year (balance after year \(n-1\)) Interest for year \(n\) (\(0.04\, a_{n-1}\)) Balance after year \(n\)
\(n = 1\) £100.00 (\(a_0\)) £4.00 £104.00 (\(a_1\))
\(n = 2\) £104.00 (\(a_1\)) £4.16 £108.16 (\(a_2\))
\(n = 3\) £108.16 (\(a_2\)) £4.33 £112.49 (\(a_3\))

Example 2: Tower of Hanoi

Setup: Three pegs, and \(n\) discs of different sizes stacked on peg 1 from largest at the bottom to smallest at the top.

The goal is to move the whole stack to another peg, moving one disc at a time and never placing a larger disc on a smaller one.

Let \(T(n)\) be the number of moves used by the best strategy.

  • One disc needs one move, so \(T(1) = 1\).

  • Recurrence, obtained by looking at the largest disc:

    1. Move the top \(n-1\) discs to the spare peg.
      • This takes \(T(n-1)\) moves.
    2. Move the largest disc to the target peg.
      • This takes one move.
    3. Move the \(n-1\) discs from the spare peg onto the largest disc.
      • This takes \(T(n-1)\) moves.

    Hence \(T(n) = 2T(n-1) + 1\) for \(n \geq 2\).

  • Small values: \(T(2) = 3\) and \(T(3) = 7\), which matches the seven moves needed for three discs.
  • Closed form: \(T(n) = 2^n - 1\). Verification:
    • Initial value (Task 1): \(2^1 - 1 = 1\). ✓
    • Recurrence (Task 2): \(2(2^{n-1} - 1) + 1 = 2^n - 1\). ✓
  • Caveat: the recurrence counts the moves of this particular strategy.
    • Showing that no strategy can do better is a separate argument, which the course material develops elsewhere.

Example 3: Climbing stairs

Setup: Each move climbs either one step or two steps.

Let \(a_n\) be the number of different ways to climb \(n\) steps.

  • \(a_1 = 1\), since the only option is a single step.
    • \(a_2 = 2\), since the options are one step then one step, or one double step.
  • Consider the final move.
    • It is either a single step from step \(n-1\) or a double step from step \(n-2\).
    • These two cases cannot happen at the same time, so the counts add: \(a_n = a_{n-1} + a_{n-2}\) for \(n \geq 3\).
  • The first terms are \(a_3 = 3\), \(a_4 = 5\), \(a_5 = 8\), \(a_6 = 13\) and \(a_7 = 21\).
  • The recurrence is the same as the one defining the Fibonacci numbers.
    • Only the initial values differ, so the sequence is a shifted Fibonacci sequence.

Example 4 : Call Count of Naive Recursive Fibonacci

Let \(C(n)\) be the number of function calls made when the \(n\) -th Fibonacci number is computed by the straightforward recursive definition.

Each call with \(n \geq 2\) makes one call for \(n-1\) and one for \(n-2\), and it counts itself. So:

  • \(C(n) = C(n-1) + C(n-2) + 1\) for \(n \geq 2\), with \(C(0) = C(1) = 1\).
  • Values: \(C(2) = 3\), \(C(3) = 5\), \(C(4) = 9\).
    • These can be checked by drawing the call tree for each case.
  • The count grows exponentially.
    • This is the kind of result that complexity analysis is designed to expose, and it is why storing previously computed values (memoisation, or computing bottom-up) is so much cheaper.

Complexity analysis

Recurrences are central to analysing algorithms.

Suppose \(f(n)\) is the complexity of an algorithm on an input of size \(n\).

Very often the work for size \(n+1\) is expressed in terms of the work for smaller sizes, such as \(f(n)\) and \(f(n-1)\).

Turning that relation into a closed form gives an explicit bound on the running time, which can then be compared across algorithms.

  • The Fibonacci example above follows this pattern.
