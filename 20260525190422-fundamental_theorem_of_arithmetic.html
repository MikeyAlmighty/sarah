Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic (Prime Factorization)

Statement

Every integer \(n > 1\) can be written as a product of primes, and this factorisation is unique (up to ordering):

\[ n = p_1^{e_1} \cdot p_2^{e_2} \cdots p_k^{e_k} \]

Proof

Existence

Every integer \(n > 1\) is either prime itself, or can be factorised into smaller factors. Repeatedly factorising those factors eventually yields at least one prime factorisation.

Uniqueness (by contradiction)

Assume \(n\) is the smallest integer \(> 1\) with two distinct prime factorisations:

\[ n = p_1^{e_1} \cdot p_2^{e_2} \cdots p_k^{e_k} = q_1^{f_1} \cdot q_2^{f_2} \cdots q_\ell^{f_\ell} \]

Step 1: All \(p_i\) are distinct from all \(q_j\). If any \(p_i = q_j\), we could divide \(n\) by it and obtain a smaller integer with two prime factorisations, contradicting minimality.

Step 2: Assume primes are listed in increasing order and \(k, \ell \geq 2\) (otherwise \(n\) is a prime power). Write:

\[ n = p_1 \cdot P = q_1 \cdot Q \]

where \(P > p_1\) and \(Q > q_1\).

Step 3: Subtract \(p_1 q_1\) from all parts:

\[ n - p_1 q_1 = p_1(P - q_1) = q_1(Q - p_1) \]

Step 4: Since both \(n\) and \(p_1 q_1\) are multiples of \(p_1\), so is \(n - p_1 q_1\). Similarly it is a multiple of \(q_1\). Therefore \(n - p_1 q_1\) can be written in two different ways as a product of integers, and:

\[ n - p_1 q_1 \geq p_1 > 1 \]

So \(n - p_1 q_1\) is smaller than \(n\) with two distinct prime factorisations — contradicting that \(n\) was the smallest such number.

Connection to Divisibility

The uniqueness of prime factorisation underpins the theory of divisibility in ℤ — it guarantees that primes are the true "building blocks" of the integers, and that factorisation into them is unambiguous.

Example

Find the prime factorisation of \(1820\).

Method: Trial Division

We start by checking whether \(2\), the smallest prime, divides \(1820\):

\[ 1820 = 2 \cdot 910 \]

Next we check whether \(2\) divides \(910\):

\[ 910 = 2 \cdot 455 \]

Since \(455\) is not divisible by \(2\), we know that \(1820 = 2^2 \cdot 455\) and that \(1820\) has no more factors of \(2\).

Moving to the next prime, \(3\): since \(455\) is not divisible by \(3\), neither is \(1820\).

Next we check \(5\):

\[ 455 = 5 \cdot 91 \]

So \(1820 = 2^2 \cdot 5 \cdot 91\).

Continuing, \(91 = 7 \cdot 13\), where \(13\) is itself prime.

Result

This gives us the full factorisation:

\[ 1820 = 2^2 \cdot 5 \cdot 7 \cdot 13 \]
